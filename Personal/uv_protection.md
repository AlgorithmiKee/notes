# UV Protection

## Abstract

This article explains the necessity of ultraviolet (UV) protection and gives practical guide on UV protection. UV radiation from the sun can cause skin damage, premature aging, and increase the risk of skin cancer. Therefore, UV protection is crutial for maintaining skin health.

## UV Light

UV light is a type of electromagnetic wave emitted by the sun.

UV light reaching the Earth's surface is divided into three categories by wavelength: UVA, UVB, and UVC.

* UVA (320-400 nm): UVA rays penetrate deep into the skin and can cause premature aging and wrinkles. Accumulated exposure to UVA rays increases the risk of skin cancer.
* UVB (290-320 nm): UVB rays have a shorter wavelength and less penetration than UVA rays, but they are the primary cause of sunburn and the main driver of skin cancer.
* UVC (100-290 nm): UVC rays have the shortest wavelength and most damaging potential, but they are mostly absorbed by the Earth's atmosphere and negligible at the surface.

As the wavelength decreases, the energy of the photons increases while the penetration depth decreases.

### Interaction of UV Rays with Matters

**Interaction of UV rays with atmosphere:**
UV rays interact with the Earth's atmosphere, where they can be absorbed or scattered by molecules and particles. The ozone layer particularly absorbs most (nearly 100%) of the harmful UVC and a significant portion (about 95%) of UVB rays. However, the atmosphere filters only about 5% of UVA rays, allowing them to reach the Earth's surface.

The intensity of UVB is highly dependent on the angle of the sun. In midday, UVB rays travels a shorter path through the atmosphere and are thus more intense compared to early morning or late afternoon. In contrast, UVA rays are less affected by the angle of the sun and maintain a more consistent intensity throughout the day.

**Interaction of UV rays with skin:**
The short-wavelength UVB rays reaches only the epidermis. It helps the synthesis of vitamin D but also contributes to sunburn. UVB rays are particularly effective at causing DNA damage, which increases the risk of skin cancer. Although UVA rays is less energetic, they can penetrate deeper into the dermis and damage collagen and elastin fibers, leading to tanning, premature aging, and wrinkles.

**Interaction of UV rays with reflective surfaces:**
Both UVA and UVB rays follow the law of reflection. However, UVB rays are more pronounced in diffuse reflection, while UVA rays are more likely to be reflected specularly. Surfaces appearing smooth for UVA rays may appear rough for UVB rays. Diffuse reflection of UVB rays can increase the overall UV exposure, especially in environments with reflective surfaces such as snow, sand, or water.

**Interaction of UV rays with glass:**
Most common glass types, such as window glass, can block UVB rays but allow UVA rays to pass through. Intuitively, standard window glass appears opaque to UVB rays, but it is transparent to UVA rays and visible light. Therefore, UVA protection is necessary even indoors or in cars.

### UV Index

The UV index quantifies the intensity of UV radiation, defined by

$$
\text{UVI} = k_\text{er} \int_{250 \text{ nm}}^{400 \text{ nm}} E(\lambda) \cdot S_\text{er}(\lambda) \, d\lambda
$$

where

* $E(\lambda)$ is the spectral irradiance $[\text{W} / (\text{m}^2 \cdot \text{nm})]$ of UV radiation at wavelength $\lambda$. It represents the power of UV radiation per unit area and per unit wavelength. The spectral irradiance varies with factors such as time of day, season, latitude, and atmospheric conditions.
* $S_\text{er}(\lambda)$ is the erythemal action spectrum, a dimensionless weighting factor that represents the sensitivity of human skin to UV radiation at wavelengths $\lambda$. It peaks around 297 nm, putting more weight on UVB rays than UVA rays.
* $k_\text{er} = 40 \, \text{m}^2 / \text{W}$ is a normalization constant to make the UV index a dimensionless quantity ranging from 0 to 11+.

The UV index is categorized as follows:

| UV Index | Risk Level | Protection Measures                          |
|----------|------------|----------------------------------------------|
| 0-2      | Low        | Minimal protection needed                    |
| 3-5      | Moderate   | Seek shade during midday                     |
| 6-7      | High       | UV protection needed from this level onwards |
| 8-10     | Very High  | Plus: Avoid sun exposure during midday       |
| 11+      | Extreme    | Plus: Avoid sun exposure                     |

The erythemal action spectrum $S_\text{er}(\lambda)$ is heavily skewed towards UVB rays. Hence, the UV index is good at quantifying the risk of immediate sunburn, but it undervalues the long-term effects of UVA rays. Therefore, even when the UV index is low, it is still important to protect against UVA rays. The table above is rather a necessary condition for UV protection.

## Sunscreen

Sunscreen protects the skin from UV rays mainly by two mechanisms: mineral filters and organic filters. Both types of filters prevent UV rays from penetrating the skin and causing damage by absorbing UV photons and converting them into heat. Most sunscreens contain a combination of both filters to provide a balance among broad-spectrum protection, skin feel, and cosmetics.

**Mineral filters**: Mineral filters are fine mineral powders such as $\text{TiO}_2$ and $\text{ZnO}$. They are effective against both UVA and UVB rays and are very unlikely to cause skin irritation. The drawbacks are (1) that they can leave white residual on the skin, and (2) that they may feel heavy if the sunscreen is not properly formulated.

**Organic filters**: Organic filters are organic compounds such as avobenzone, oxybenzone, and octocrylene. They feel generally lighter on the skin and less prone to white residual. The skin suitability of organic filters varies among individuals. Moreover, prolonged exposure to sunlight can degrade some organic filters.

Sunscreens also contains other non-filtering ingredients such as film-forming agents, hydrators, and preservatives. Film-forming agents help the sunscreen adhere to the skin and form a uniform protective layer. Emollients provide hydration and improve the texture of the sunscreen. Preservatives prevent microbial growth and extend the shelf life of the product.

### Rating of Sunscreen

The UVB protection of a sunscreen is typically rated by Sun Protection Factor (SPF). It is a numerical value ranging from 15 to 50+. The SPF value indicates how much longer it takes for UVB rays to cause sunburn when using the sunscreen compared to not using any protection.

$$
\text{SPF} = \frac{\text{Min. UVB dose to cause sunburn w. protection}}{\text{Min. UVB dose to cause sunburn w.o. protection}}
$$

For example, if it takes 30 minutes for the skin to turn red without any protection, then using a sunscreen with SPF 30 would theoretically allow for 30 minutes $\times$ 30 = 900 minutes (15 hours) of sun exposure before sunburn occurs.

The UVA protection of a sunscreen is typically rated by Protection Grade of UVA (PA). It is denoted by a plus sign system, where more plus signs indicate higher UVA protection. The PA rating is based on the Persistent Pigment Darkening (PPD) method, which measures the skin's ability to tan after UVA exposure.

$$
\text{PPD} = \frac{\text{Min. UVA dose to cause tanning w. protection}}{\text{Min. UVA dose to cause tanning w.o. protection}}
$$

The PPD values are categorized into PA ratings as follows:

| PPD Value | PA Rating | Protection Level |
|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| 2-4       | PA+       | Low              |
| 4-8       | PA++      | Moderate         |
| 8-16      | PA+++     | High             |
| 16+       | PA++++    | Extremely high   |

Both SPF and PPD are defined as ratios of minimum doses. The actual percentage of blocked UV rays can be calculated by

$$
b = \left(1 - \frac{1}{r}\right) \times 100\%
$$

where $r$ is either the SPF or the PPD value and $b$ is the percentage of blocked UVA (if $r$ is PPD) or UVB rays (if $r$ is SPF).

*Proof*: Let $D_0$ denote the min. dose of UV rays to cause damage (either sunburn or tanning) without protection, and $D$ denote the min. dose of UV rays to cause damage with protection. By definition of SPF or PPD, we have

$$
r = \frac{D}{D_0}
$$

The min. dose of UV rays reaching the skin to cause damage must be the same regardless of protection. Hence,

$$
D_0 = D \cdot \left(1 - \frac{b}{100}\right)
$$

Combining the two equations, we conclude. $\quad\blacksquare$

The percentage of blocked UVB rays and SPF values are related as follows:

| SPF Value | % of UVB Blocked |
|-----------|------------------|
| 5         | 80.0%            |
| 15        | 93.3%            |
| 30        | 96.7%            |
| 50        | 98.0%            |

The percentage of blocked UVA rays and PA ratings are related as follows:

| PA Rating | PPD range | % of UVA Blocked |
|-----------|-----------|------------------|
| PA+       | 2-4       | 50-75%           |
| PA++      | 4-8       | 75-87.5%         |
| PA+++     | 8-16      | 87.5-93.75%      |
| PA++++    | 16+       | 93.75%+          |

### Choosing the Right Sunscreen

When choosing a sunscreen, the following factors should be considered:

* Broad-spectrum protection: Choose a sunscreen that offers broad-spectrum protection against both UVA and UVB rays. The SPF and PA ratings can help you determine the level of protection.

  | Scenario            | SPF | PA     |
  |---------------------|-----|--------|
  | Daily commute       | 30  | PA+++  |
  | Indoor near windows | 30  | PA+++  |
  | Outdoor activities  | 50  | PA++++ |

* Skin type: For dry skin, look for hydrating ingredients and avoid strictly matte formulas that may cause pilling. For oily skin, look for non-comedogenic and oil-free formulas that won't clog pores.

* Texture and finish: Choose a sunscreen with a texture and finish that you find comfortable. For example, if you prefer a lightweight feel, opt for a gel or fluid formula. If you want a matte finish, look for sunscreens labeled as *matte* or *oil-absorbing*.

* Water resistance: If you plan to swim or sweat, choose a water-resistant sunscreen to maintain protection. Note that reapplication is still necessary even for water-resistant sunscreens.

* Tinting: Tinted sunscreens can alleviate the white cast caused by mineral filters. They also provide additional protection against visible light.

### Correct Use of Sunscreen

To achieve the rated protection, sunscreen must be applied correctly.

**Timing**: Sunscreen should be applied at best 15 minutes before sun exposure to allow the film to form on the skin. Other skincare products, such as moisturizers, should be applied before sunscreen. Ensure that those products are fully absorbed before applying sunscreen.

$$
\boxed{\text{Skincare products}} \xrightarrow{\text{absorption}} \boxed{\text{Sunscreen}} \xrightarrow{\text{film formation}} \boxed{\text{Sun exposure}}
$$

**Apply generously**: The general recommendation is to apply about 2 mg of sunscreen per square centimeter of skin. For an average adult, this translates to about 2.5 ml for full face and neck coverage, and about 30 ml for full body coverage. Reapplication is necessary after swimming, sweating, or towel drying.

**Gentle around eyes**: The skin around the eyes is particularly thin and sensitive. Make sure the sunscreen is eye-safe before applying. Dab the sunscreen gently around the eyes. Avoid rubbing the sunscreen in the eye area.
